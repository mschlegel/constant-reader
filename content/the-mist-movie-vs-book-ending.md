*Spoilers follow for both the novella and the 2007 film, including the endings.*

Stephen King's *[The Mist](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=The+Mist+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)* and Frank Darabont's 2007 film of the same name tell almost the same story for almost their entire runtime — the same grocery store, the same monsters in the fog, the same descent into religious hysteria among the trapped survivors. Then, in its final minutes, the film veers hard away from the source material and lands on an ending King didn't write, one that's become one of the most talked-about gut-punches in modern horror movies. Here's what changes, and why.

## The source material

*The Mist* started life as a novella, first published in the 1980 horror anthology *Dark Forces* and later collected in King's 1985 short fiction collection *[Skeleton Crew](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Skeleton+Crew+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)*. The premise: after a violent storm rolls through a small Maine town, a strange, dense mist rolls in behind it, and the people trapped inside a local supermarket — including illustrator David Drayton and his young son Billy — discover that the mist is full of creatures that don't belong to this world. The novella spends most of its length on the group dynamics inside the store, particularly the rise of Mrs. Carmody, a fire-and-brimstone customer who convinces a growing faction of survivors that the mist is divine punishment requiring a human sacrifice to lift.

Frank Darabont — who'd already adapted King twice before, with *[The Shawshank Redemption](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=The+Shawshank+Redemption+Blu-ray&tag=YOURTAG-20)* and *The Green Mile* — wrote and directed the film version, released November 21, 2007, with Thomas Jane as David, Marcia Gay Harden as Mrs. Carmody, Laurie Holden as Amanda Dunfrey, Toby Jones as Ollie Weeks, and Andre Braugher as neighbor Brent Norton. For most of its two hours, it's a faithful, well-cast adaptation. The ending is where it stops being one.

## Where the mist actually comes from

Both versions gesture at the same origin: a nearby military facility called the Arrowhead Project, rumored to be experimenting with peering into other dimensions. In King's novella this is kept as background rumor — characters trade half-informed theories about it, and the story never confirms exactly what happened. The film is more direct about it, having characters state outright that the military tore open something it shouldn't have, though even the finished film stops short of showing the breach on-screen (an opening prologue depicting it was written but ultimately cut). Neither version fully resolves what's on the other side of the mist, which is intentional — this is cosmic horror, and the unknowability of the threat is the point.

## Mrs. Carmody: the same fate, played straighter on screen

One thing the film actually preserves closely from the book is what happens to Mrs. Carmody. In both versions, she works the terrified crowd into a religious frenzy and pushes for a human sacrifice — targeting Billy at one point — before Ollie Weeks shoots her dead, first in the stomach and then, as she remains upright, in the head. Her death breaks the spell over her followers almost immediately in both the novella and the film; the mob that had been ready to kill for her snaps out of it the moment she's gone. Darabont didn't need to change this beat — it already worked as written, and the film plays it for exactly the same catharsis King intended, with Harden's performance making Carmody more actively menacing than the novella's.

## The novella's ending: ambiguous, and a little hopeful

Once the survivors decide to make a run for it, the book follows David, Billy, and Amanda Dunfrey (the schoolteacher who becomes David's ally and, implicitly, more) as they escape the store and drive out into the mist-covered countryside, low on gas and with no clear destination. On the radio, they catch a fragmented transmission repeating a single word — "Hartford" — suggesting there might be a functioning refuge somewhere ahead. The novella ends with the three of them still driving, the fate of the word "Hartford" and everyone in the car left completely unresolved. King has said in interviews that he intentionally left the door open for a sequel he never wrote. It's a bleak, exhausted ending, but not a hopeless one — the last image is of people still trying.

## The film's ending: what actually happens

Darabont's script follows the same escape beat for beat, with one significant addition: before running, the group stops at David's house and finds his wife dead. From there, David, Billy, Amanda, Dan Miller, and Irene Reppler pile into his car and drive until they run out of gas, stranded with the mist and its creatures closing in and no way to keep moving.

Facing what looks like a certain, likely gruesome death, David uses his gun — which has four bullets left for five people — to kill Dan, Irene, Amanda, and finally his own son, sparing them from the mist's creatures. He has no bullet left for himself, so he climbs out of the car to be killed instead, screaming for whatever's out there to take him. Instead, the mist is lifting, and a column of military vehicles rolls out of the fog, rescuing other survivors along the road. The rescue arrived only minutes after David killed everyone in the car, including his son — meaning the family that stayed behind and waited it out, and the strangers David sees being pulled to safety along the highway, all survived doing exactly what he'd just decided wasn't possible.

## Why King actually prefers the movie's ending

King has said repeatedly, including directly to Darabont, that he likes the film's ending better than his own — a rare thing for him to say about an adaptation. His reasoning, as he's explained it in interviews, is that the film's ending has an "anti-Hollywood, nihilistic" quality that the novella's more open conclusion doesn't reach for; Darabont takes the story's cosmic-horror premise — that the universe is indifferent and human effort doesn't guarantee anything — and follows it all the way to its worst possible logical conclusion, rather than pulling back at the last moment. Darabont has said he knew he had King's blessing on the change before he shot it, which mattered to him given how different it was from the source material.

Critics and audiences responded to it as intended — reports from opening weekend in 2007 described theaters audibly gasping, and some even applauding, at the twist, and it's stayed one of the most-cited "worst ending" and "best ending" debates in horror discussion ever since, depending who you ask. Even King fans who find the scene devastating tend to agree it's an effective piece of filmmaking; whether it's a good ending is almost a separate question from whether it works.

## The black-and-white version

Darabont has said he originally wanted to shoot *The Mist* in black and white, citing *Night of the Living Dead* and the stop-motion creature-feature era as influences, but the studio wouldn't sign off on a black-and-white theatrical release. He got his way on home video: the DVD release included a black-and-white cut of the full film, which Darabont has called his preferred version — it leans harder into the movie's B-movie monster-picture roots and, according to Darabont, makes the visual effects read as more convincing by removing color-based distraction.

## The short-lived TV series

There's a third version worth knowing about, if only so you don't confuse it with the film: a *The Mist* television series aired on Spike TV starting June 2017, relocating the story to a new town and expanding it well past the grocery-store setting with an ensemble of mostly new characters. It kept little beyond the title and basic premise, was not well received, and was cancelled after a single ten-episode season due to weak ratings. If you're looking for the story you know from the book or the Darabont film, the show isn't it.

## Which version should you experience first?

If you can only pick one, the 2007 film is the more complete, more devastating experience — Darabont's direction, the ensemble cast, and that ending have made it a genuine horror classic in its own right, arguably a better-known and more acclaimed piece of work than the source novella. But the book is worth reading afterward specifically because its ending is so different: it's a quieter, more grinding kind of dread, built on the idea that endurance and hope are worth something even when you can't confirm they'll pay off — the exact quality the film's ending is built to demolish. Reading both is really reading two different arguments about the same premise, and each makes the other hit harder.
