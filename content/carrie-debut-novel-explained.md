Published on April 5, 1974, by Doubleday, *[Carrie](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Carrie+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)* is Stephen King's first novel — and one of the unlikeliest debuts in modern horror, given that King almost threw it away before it was finished. It's short by his later standards, barely over 200 pages, but it already has the two things that would define his career: an ordinary setting turned lethal, and a genuine, uncomfortable empathy for the person causing the carnage.

## How the book came to exist

King started *Carrie* as a short story intended for *Cavalier*, a men's magazine that had bought some of his earlier work. He based the title character partly on two girls he'd known in high school — both isolated, both from strict households, both treated badly by classmates in ways that stuck with him. A few pages in, he decided he didn't understand teenage girls or the mechanics of a girls' locker room well enough to keep going, and threw the draft in the trash.

His wife, Tabitha King, fished it out, read it, and told him it was worth finishing — she thought the premise was strong and offered to help him get the details right. That rescued draft became the novel. King has told the story many times, including in his memoir *[On Writing](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=On+Writing+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)*, and it's become one of the more famous "nearly didn't happen" anecdotes in publishing — not just because the book got finished, but because the money from it changed his life. He was teaching high school English and barely scraping by when Doubleday bought the novel for a modest advance; the paperback rights then sold for $400,000, more money than he'd ever seen, and it let him quit teaching and write full-time.

## The setup

*Carrie* is set in the fictional town of Chamberlain, Maine, and centers on Carrie White, a withdrawn, friendless high school junior raised by her mother, Margaret, a fanatical religious extremist who treats ordinary adolescence — makeup, dances, her daughter's own body — as sin. The book opens with its most notorious scene: Carrie gets her first period in the girls' locker room after gym class, at nearly seventeen years old, with no idea what's happening to her because Margaret never told her. Her classmates respond by throwing tampons and pads at her, chanting "plug it up," while Carrie, convinced she's bleeding to death, has no framework for understanding any of it.

That public humiliation is also the moment her telekinesis fully awakens — King suggests the stress of puberty is what triggers it, tying the power directly to the biology her mother has spent her whole life treating as shameful. Carrie has had small, barely-noticed flashes of the ability since childhood, but from the locker room scene forward, it grows fast.

## The structure that makes it unusual

Unlike almost anything else in King's catalog, *Carrie* isn't told as a straightforward narrative. It's assembled after the fact, as a kind of documentary record: excerpts from a book called *My Name Is Susan Snell* written by a survivor, newspaper clippings, magazine articles, and sworn testimony from "The White Commission," a fictional state investigation into the disaster that came to be known as "the Black Prom." The reader is told from the first pages that something catastrophic happens, and the tension comes from watching the pieces assemble rather than wondering whether disaster is coming at all.

This matters because it shapes how sympathetic the book is willing to be. Interspersed with the destruction, the reader keeps getting outside perspectives — official, clinical, retrospective — that insist on treating Carrie's rampage as a documented tragedy rather than a monster story. It's a structural choice King never fully returned to later in his career, and it's part of why the novel still reads as distinct even among his other outsider-revenge stories.

## What happens at prom

Guilt-ridden over her own role in the locker room incident, Sue Snell — one of the girls who threw tampons at Carrie — convinces her boyfriend Tommy Ross to ask Carrie to the senior prom instead of taking Sue herself, hoping to give Carrie one normal, kind experience. Carrie, suspicious but increasingly hopeful, says yes, and for most of the night, the gesture works: she and Tommy are elected prom king and queen, and for the first time in the book, Carrie feels like she belongs somewhere.

That's exactly the setup for the novel's central cruelty. Chris Hargenson, the ringleader of the locker room bullying, has been expelled from prom over the incident and wants revenge. With her boyfriend Billy Nolan, she rigs the vote and plants two buckets of pig's blood above the stage, timed to dump over Carrie the moment she's crowned. It happens on camera, in front of the entire school, at the exact moment Carrie has let herself feel accepted. Tommy is accidentally killed by the falling bucket, and something in Carrie breaks completely.

What follows is the full unleashing of her power: she locks the gymnasium doors, electrocutes and burns much of the graduating class, and walks out into Chamberlain itself, setting fire to gas stations and destroying a meaningful portion of the town on her way home. The novel's death toll runs into the hundreds — far beyond what any film adaptation has shown, largely for budget and tone reasons.

## The ending, explained

Carrie returns home to Margaret, who has always viewed her daughter's powers as proof of demonic influence and has been quietly preparing to kill her rather than let that sin continue. Margaret stabs Carrie in the shoulder with a kitchen knife, and Carrie, in retaliation and out of sheer reflex, stops her mother's heart with her mind. It's the book's bleakest moment precisely because it isn't framed as triumphant — Carrie doesn't want to kill her mother, and does it anyway, almost as an extension of the same power that's already killed dozens of people she had no grievance against.

Mortally wounded, Carrie crawls to find Sue Snell, the one person who tried to do something decent for her. Carrie reads Sue's mind in her final moments and understands, for certain, that Sue hadn't been part of the prom-night cruelty — that her kindness had been real. She dies in the street outside, and the destroyed town is left to the Commission investigators to piece together after the fact, which is the "record" the reader has been assembling the whole novel. In an epilogue, a letter suggests another girl, far away, may have inherited a similar latent ability — a quiet, unresolved note that the horror isn't really contained just because Carrie is gone.

## What the book is actually about

Underneath the telekinesis, *Carrie* is a book about what happens when cruelty is allowed to compound unchecked — by classmates, by a parent, and by a town that looks away. Margaret White isn't a cartoonish villain so much as an extreme, curdled version of a real phenomenon: a parent who conflates control with love and treats her child's normal development as a threat to manage rather than support. The bullying at school isn't separate from that; it's the same dynamic at a different scale, kids punishing Carrie for being strange in ways her home life guaranteed she would be.

King has said he intended the novel partly as commentary on how American culture treats women's bodies and sexuality — the shame around menstruation, the policing of girls' behavior, the particular public cruelty reserved for women who don't fit in — and that reading holds up; it's a big part of why the book still feels sharp rather than dated. It's also, notably, a rare example in King's early work of a "monster" the reader is meant to grieve for rather than simply fear. Carrie is a victim for almost the entire novel before she becomes dangerous, and King never lets the reader fully forget that the second half is the direct, ugly result of the first.

## The adaptations, briefly

*Carrie* has been adapted more times than almost any other King property: a 1976 theatrical film directed by Brian De Palma and starring Sissy Spacek, a 2002 TV movie with Angela Bettis, a 2013 remake starring Chloë Grace Moretz, and even a short-lived Broadway musical. Each handles the ending differently — most notably the 2002 version, which lets Carrie survive — and none fully replicates the novel's documentary-style structure. ## Should you read it?

Yes, and it's one of the easier King books to commit to — short, fast-moving, and a genuinely useful look at where his lifelong interests (small-town cruelty, religious extremism, the outsider pushed too far) started. It's rawer and less polished than his later work, but that rawness is part of its appeal; you can feel a writer figuring out, in real time, what he's good at.
