Stephen King's books are long, and a good chunk of them were written to be read aloud in your head anyway — first-person asides, Maine dialect, a narrator who occasionally winks at the reader. That makes him one of the best-suited authors in fiction for audiobooks, and Simon & Schuster and Random House Audio have generally matched him with narrators good enough to make 30-, 40-, even 47-hour listens feel earned rather than exhausting.

The catch is that King's audiobook catalog is uneven, partly because of history outside anyone's control — his longtime narrator of choice was in a motorcycle accident in 2001 that ended his recording career, forcing a handoff mid-series. Here's which audiobooks are genuinely great, which narrators to look for by name, and where the handoffs happened.

## The narrators who define the King audio experience

Before ranking individual titles, it helps to know the handful of names that show up again and again, because at this point they're as much a part of the experience as King's prose.

**Frank Muller** was King's narrator of choice through the 1990s and is still the name longtime listeners bring up first. He narrated *[The Green Mile](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=The+Green+Mile+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)*, *Desperation*, and the first four *Dark Tower* novels (*The Gunslinger*, *The Drawing of the Three*, *The Waste Lands*, and *Wizard and Glass*), among others, with a gravelly, unhurried delivery that a lot of fans consider the definitive King voice. In 2001, Muller suffered severe brain injuries in a motorcycle accident that ended his acting and narration career; he died in 2008. King himself was visibly affected by it — he organized benefit readings for Muller's medical costs — and the accident is the reason several of King's series have a narrator seam partway through.

**George Guidall** picked up the *Dark Tower* series after Muller's accident, narrating *Wolves of the Calla*, *Song of Susannah*, and *The Dark Tower* itself. It's a noticeably different voice from Muller's, which some listeners find jarring mid-series, but Guidall — one of the most prolific audiobook narrators in the business, with well over a thousand titles to his name — handles the tonal shift of the back half of the series (more urgency, less mystery) well.

**Will Patton** is the closest thing King's had to a Muller successor, and arguably his best working narrator today. Patton has voiced the Bill Hodges trilogy (*Mr. Mercedes*, *Finders Keepers*, *End of Watch*), *Doctor Sleep*, *The Outsider*, *If It Bleeds*, *Holly*, and *You Like It Darker*, among others. He's got a raspy, weathered voice that suits King's older-detective and small-town-menace material especially well, and he's genuinely gifted at differentiating a large cast without resorting to cartoonish voices.

**Grover Gardner** narrated the widely available unabridged edition of *[The Stand](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=The+Stand+Complete+and+Uncut+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)*, including the Complete and Uncut edition, and does an excellent job carrying an ensemble cast across nearly 48 hours without losing momentum. **Craig Wasson** narrated *[11/22/63](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=11%2F22%2F63+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)*, giving Jake Epping's Maine flatness and the novel's period-Texas voices real distinction. **Steven Weber** narrated *[It](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=It+Stephen+King+audiobook&tag=YOURTAG-20)*, and it's become the benchmark performance of Pennywise outside the films — Weber voices seven kids, their adult selves, and a shape-shifting entity across nearly 45 hours without the distinctions ever blurring together.

## The best individual audiobooks

**1. It, narrated by Steven Weber.** The single most acclaimed King audiobook. At just under 45 hours it's a massive commitment, but Weber's performance is the reason people recommend the audio version of *It* over the print edition specifically — his Pennywise voice work has become its own point of reference. See our [full plot summary and ending explainer](/it-plot-summary-ending-explained/) if you want the story beats going in.

**2. The Green Mile, narrated by Frank Muller.** Muller's performance is frequently cited as his best King work, and it suits the material — the novel was originally released in six serialized parts, and Muller's patient, storytelling cadence matches that structure's slow-reveal pacing better than a faster narrator would.

**3. The Stand (Complete and Uncut), narrated by Grover Gardner.** Nearly 48 hours long, but Gardner's steady hand across dozens of characters and a genuinely apocalyptic scope makes it one of the few audiobooks that can carry that length without dragging. If you're deciding which edition to actually read or listen to, our [guide to the uncut edition vs. the original](/the-stand-uncut-edition-vs-original/) breaks down what's different.

**4. 11/22/63, narrated by Craig Wasson.** A 30-plus hour listen that benefits enormously from being read aloud — Wasson's handling of the period-accurate Texas accents and Lee Harvey Oswald's household gives the book's slow-burn tension more texture than it has on the page.

**5. Doctor Sleep, narrated by Will Patton.** Patton's take on adult Danny Torrance is one of his best performances, and the audiobook is a good way to experience the *[Shining](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=The+Shining+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)* sequel if you've already read the original in print and want a different way into the follow-up.

**6. Mr. Mercedes, narrated by Will Patton.** The start of the Bill Hodges trilogy, and a strong entry point if you want to sample Patton's work before committing to a longer King audiobook — it's a tighter, faster thriller than most of King's catalog, which keeps a first listen from feeling like a marathon.

**7. Pet Sematary, narrated by Michael C. Hall.** A slightly different pairing from the usual King-audiobook regulars — Hall's controlled, clipped delivery fits the novel's creeping dread well, and the casting (post-*Dexter*) was a deliberate match of a performer known for restrained menace to a book that depends on it.

**8. Salem's Lot, narrated by Ron McLarty.** McLarty's narration leans into the small-town-Maine atmosphere that makes the novel work, and he's particularly good at the slow accumulation of dread across the book's first half, before the vampire threat is fully out in the open.

## Which format actually matters here

A recurring theme in the list above: King's audiobooks tend to earn their length rather than merely justify it. His prose already reads like someone telling you a story rather than writing one at you, and a strong narrator amplifies that rather than fighting it. If you're new to King on audio, the safest recommendations are *It* for the single most famous performance, or *Mr. Mercedes* if you want something shorter to test whether audio is the right format for you before committing 40-plus hours to a *Dark Tower* or *Stand*-sized listen.

One practical note if you're buying rather than using a subscription credit: physical and Kindle editions are usually cheaper per book than a one-off audiobook purchase, so audio makes the most sense if you're already paying for a service like Audible or your library's Libby app rather than buying titles individually. Either way, the print editions are worth having on the shelf regardless — you can find most of the titles above, including [It](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=It+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20) and [Doctor Sleep](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Doctor+Sleep+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20), in whichever format suits you.

## The short version

For the single best King audiobook, start with *It* (Steven Weber). For the deepest catalog cut, *The Green Mile* (Frank Muller) is the one longtime listeners point to as underrated. And if you want to sample a narrator before committing to a 40-hour book, Will Patton's Bill Hodges trilogy is the easiest entry point working today.
