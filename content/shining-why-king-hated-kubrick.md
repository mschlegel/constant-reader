Stanley Kubrick's *The Shining* (1980) is routinely ranked among the greatest horror films ever made. Stephen King has never agreed, and he's said so, repeatedly and specifically, for more than four decades. This isn't a case of an author making one grumpy remark and moving on — King's criticism of the film has been consistent since it premiered, detailed enough that he eventually wrote and produced his own three-part miniseries in 1997 specifically to correct it. Here's what he actually objected to, and why the disagreement runs as deep as it does.

## The novel Kubrick was working from

Published in 1977, *[The Shining](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=The+Shining+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)* is King's third novel and, by his own account, his most personal — written while he was struggling with alcoholism himself, about a recovering alcoholic named Jack Torrance who takes a winter caretaker job at the isolated Overlook Hotel with his wife Wendy and young son Danny. The hotel is haunted and actively malevolent, and it works on Jack's specific weaknesses — his drinking, his temper, his resentment at his own failed writing career — to slowly turn him into a vehicle for its violence. Danny has psychic abilities (the "shining" of the title) that let him see the hotel's history and its designs on his father.

The critical word is *slowly*. In the book, Jack starts out as a fundamentally decent, loving father who is losing a real fight against the hotel and his own demons — and in the novel's climax, he wins a version of that fight. A fragment of Jack breaks through the hotel's control for just long enough to let his family escape, at the cost of his own life when the Overlook's faulty boiler explodes and destroys it. King has described the ending as being about a man's redemption; the horror of the book is that decent people can be undone by things partly outside their control, and its emotional payoff is that Jack still loves his family enough, underneath everything, to save them.

## Kubrick's changes, and why King objected

Kubrick's film, starring Jack Nicholson as Jack, Shelley Duvall as Wendy, and Danny Lloyd as Danny, was released in May 1980 and keeps the setting and broad premise but changes almost everything else about tone, structure, and meaning. King's objections have been remarkably consistent across interviews for over 40 years, and they center on a few specific points:

**Jack Nicholson plays crazy from the first scene.** King's most frequently repeated complaint is that Nicholson's performance gives the audience nothing to lose — he reads as unstable and vaguely threatening in the hiring interview, before the Overlook has done anything to him. That kills the arc the novel depends on: a good man's slow corruption. "Jack Torrance's slide into madness [in the book] is like watching a fuse burn down to a stick of dynamite," King has said in interviews explaining the difference; in the film, there's no fuse, just an already-lit charge.

**Wendy is written as weak.** In the BBC interview where King laid out his objections most bluntly, he said Shelley Duvall's Wendy is "basically just there to scream and be stupid, and that's not the woman that I wrote about." In the novel, Wendy is resourceful and central to the family's survival; King has said Kubrick's script reduces her to a hysterical bystander, which he's called one of the more misogynistic choices in the film.

**There's no redemption.** Kubrick's Jack dies frozen in a hedge maze (replacing the book's topiary animals, which the film's effects team couldn't make convincing), chasing Danny with an axe, with no final moment of the character breaking free of the hotel's influence. Since King has been open that the novel's ending is bound up with his own experience of addiction and the possibility of getting better, cutting the redemption arc wasn't a small trim to him — it removed the point of the story.

**The film is emotionally cold, on purpose.** Kubrick was a noted skeptic of straightforward good-and-evil morality, and by most critical readings, the film is deliberately ambiguous about whether anything supernatural is happening at all, versus Jack simply being a violent man who snaps under isolation and cabin fever. That ambiguity is a large part of why critics consider the film a masterpiece of tone — and exactly why King, whose fiction tends to be explicit about supernatural evil and about ordinary human decency as a real force against it, found it hollow. King has summarized the mismatch as Kubrick being "a very cold man" making "a horror picture with no heart."

King's most quoted lines about the film both come from around its release and after: on David Letterman's show, he said, "I feel as though I'd given Stanley Kubrick a Cadillac and he made me a bicycle" (versions of the quote vary slightly across retellings, with King also comparing the film to "a beautiful car with no engine in it") — and separately, that reading the script felt like handing Kubrick "a live grenade" that he "heroically threw his body on."

## The making-of tensions behind the scenes

Kubrick's shoot has its own well-documented reputation for being brutal, particularly for Shelley Duvall, who has said in multiple interviews that filming was one of the most physically and emotionally exhausting experiences of her career — Kubrick reportedly demanded dozens to over a hundred takes of individual scenes, including the famous baseball-bat confrontation. King has pointed to that treatment as part of why he thinks the film's Wendy comes across as merely frazzled rather than resilient: an actor pushed to genuine exhaustion doesn't necessarily read as "brave," just worn down.

Kubrick was also notoriously hands-off with King as the credited screenwriter — King's original draft was set aside, and the shooting script credited to Kubrick and novelist Diane Johnson bears only a loose resemblance to it. King wasn't consulted on set and has said he saw the film essentially as a finished product, with little say in how his book was being reshaped.

## King's own version: the 1997 miniseries

King didn't just complain — he eventually got the chance to do it his way. In 1997, ABC aired a three-part miniseries of *The Shining*, with King writing the teleplay himself and Mick Garris directing. Steven Weber played Jack Torrance and Rebecca De Mornay played Wendy, and the production filmed at the Stanley Hotel in Estes Park, Colorado — the real hotel that inspired the novel in the first place, which Kubrick's film had not used (Kubrick shot exteriors at Oregon's Timberline Lodge and built the interiors on soundstages in England).

The miniseries restores the book's slower burn, its topiary animals instead of a hedge maze, Wendy as a more capable character, and Jack's climactic moment of self-sacrifice. Critically, it was received far less warmly than Kubrick's film — reviewers generally found it competent but flat, lacking the visual command that makes Kubrick's version so unshakeable regardless of how unfaithful it is. It's a useful data point either way: King's version proves that fidelity to the book doesn't automatically produce the better film, even though it's the version King has said he actually prefers, understandably, as the one that reflects what he meant to write.

## So who's right?

Both, arguably, depending on what you're asking the story to do. Judged as an adaptation — does it carry over the book's characters, arc, and meaning — Kubrick's *Shining* is genuinely unfaithful in the ways King describes, and his objections aren't sour grapes; they're specific and largely accurate descriptions of what changed. Judged as a film on its own terms, Kubrick's version is a different, stranger work that uses the Overlook Hotel to ask questions the novel never asks, and that's a big part of why it has endured as a standalone classic independent of the book.

If you've only seen the movie, the novel is worth reading precisely because it's a different story wearing the same premise — a study of addiction and a family trying to survive it, rather than a study of dread and ambiguity. Pair it with *[Doctor Sleep](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Doctor+Sleep+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)*, King's own sequel, which had to awkwardly reconcile the fact that most readers know the Overlook from Kubrick's film rather than the book it's actually following up.
