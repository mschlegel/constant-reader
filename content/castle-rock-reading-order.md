Derry gets more attention because *It* is the size of a phone book and impossible to miss, but Castle Rock is the Stephen King town that actually has the most books set in it. It's smaller, meaner in a quieter way, and King kept coming back to it across five decades — long after he'd supposedly killed it off for good in 1991.

Unlike the Dark Tower books, these aren't a series in the sequential sense. You don't need book one to understand book two. What you get instead is a shared setting and a handful of recurring names — the Merrill family, Sheriff Alan Pangborn, the town's general unluckiness — that reward reading them with the town's history in mind. Here's every major Castle Rock work, the order that makes the most sense, and how it all fits together.

## The four core novels

These are the books that built Castle Rock into a place with its own history, and reading them in publication order is also reading them in the order King built on what came before.

**1. *[The Dead Zone](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=The+Dead+Zone+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)* (1979)**
The first King book to use Castle Rock, though it's not really *about* the town — it's about Johnny Smith, who wakes from a five-year coma with the ability to see a person's future (or past) by touching them. Castle Rock's contribution to the plot is Frank Dodd, a sheriff's deputy revealed to be a serial killer terrorizing the town, which Johnny helps expose. It's a small piece of a bigger story, but it's the seed everything else grew from.

**2. *[Cujo](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Cujo+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)* (1981)**
The first novel actually set in Castle Rock from the ground up. A friendly St. Bernard contracts rabies and traps a mother and her young son in a dead car in the summer heat. It's one of King's leanest, most brutal books — almost no supernatural element, just a town, a sick animal, and bad luck compounding on bad luck.

**3. *[The Dark Half](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=The+Dark+Half+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)* (1989)**
Technically set mostly in the neighboring town of Ludlow, where novelist Thad Beaumont lives, but it's a Castle Rock book because Alan Pangborn — who becomes the closest thing this corner of King's universe has to a recurring hero — is Castle Rock's sheriff, and he's the one investigating when Thad's pseudonym, George Stark, starts killing people in the real world. Pangborn carries over into the next two books, so this is where you want to meet him.

**4. *[Needful Things](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Needful+Things+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)* (1991)**
Subtitled *The Last Castle Rock Story* on its original release, and it earns that subtitle — a mysterious new shop opens on Main Street, run by a stranger named Leland Gaunt who sells people exactly what they've always wanted, for a price that starts small and ends with the entire town tearing itself apart. King wrote it as a deliberate finale, literally destroying Castle Rock by the last page, in part because he said he needed to stop himself from being tempted back to it. (He came back anyway.) Pangborn returns here, now worn down by what happened in *The Dark Half*, which makes this land harder if you've read that book first.

## The novellas that fill in the gaps

Two shorter works are essential to the full picture, and one of them is arguably the best writing to ever come out of Castle Rock.

**Chronologically first: *[The Body](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Different+Seasons+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)* (1982, collected in *Different Seasons*)**
Set in the summer of 1960, decades before any of the four novels above, *The Body* follows four 12-year-old boys — Gordie Lachance, Chris Chambers, Teddy Duchamp, and Vern Tessio — on a walk along the railroad tracks outside Castle Rock to find the body of a missing kid. There's nothing supernatural in it at all; it's a story about childhood, class, and the kind of friendship you only get once. It was adapted into *Stand by Me* (1986), directed by Rob Reiner and starring Wil Wheaton, River Phoenix, Corey Feldman, and Jerry O'Connell — one of the most acclaimed King adaptations ever made, though the film relocated the town to Oregon for production reasons. Reading *The Body* doesn't require any of the other Castle Rock books, but it's worth knowing Ace Merrill, the hoodlum antagonist here, is the older brother of a kid who shows up again decades later in *Needful Things*.

**Bridges directly into *Needful Things*: "The Sun Dog" (1990, collected in *Four Past Midnight*)**
A teenager's new Polaroid camera starts producing photos of a snarling dog that gets closer in every shot, no matter what the camera is actually pointed at. He brings it to Pop Merrill — Ace's father, running the town's junk shop — to figure out what's wrong with it. This novella was written specifically to set up *Needful Things*: Pop Merrill becomes one of Leland Gaunt's first and most consequential customers in that novel, so reading "The Sun Dog" right before *Needful Things* genuinely improves it.

A few shorter stories round out the town's history for completists, including "It Grows on You" (in the *Skeleton Crew* collection) and "Uncle Otto's Truck," both set in Castle Rock but not required reading to follow the main throughline.

## King's return to Castle Rock, decades later

Castle Rock stayed "destroyed" for over 25 years. Then King went back.

**The Gwendy trilogy, with Richard Chizmar:**
- *[Gwendy's Button Box](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Gwendy%27s+Button+Box+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)* (2017) — co-written with Chizmar, following a young girl in 1970s Castle Rock given a mysterious box with buttons that can do impossible — and terrible — things.
- *Gwendy's Magic Feather* (2019) — written solely by Chizmar, continuing Gwendy's story as an adult.
- *Gwendy's Final Task* (2022) — King and Chizmar back together for the full-length conclusion, which also ties the trilogy into the Dark Tower mythology.

**Elevation (2018):** A quieter, almost uplifting novella about a Castle Rock man who starts mysteriously losing weight without changing size, and the small-town prejudice he confronts along the way when he tries to help a lesbian couple struggling to open a restaurant in town. It's one of King's gentlest books, a long way removed from *Cujo* or *Needful Things* in tone, but still unmistakably Castle Rock.

## Suggested reading order

If you want the full experience, here's the order that works best:

1. *The Body* (the town before anything supernatural touches it)
2. *The Dead Zone*
3. *Cujo*
4. *The Dark Half*
5. "The Sun Dog"
6. *Needful Things*
7. *Gwendy's Button Box* → *Gwendy's Magic Feather* → *Gwendy's Final Task*
8. *Elevation*

That's internal chronology more than publication order, and it means Pangborn's arc across *The Dark Half* and *Needful Things* lands the way King intended, with "The Sun Dog" paying off immediately after. If you'd rather just pick one and go, start with *The Body* if you want King at his least horror-adjacent, or *Needful Things* if you want the full, chaotic Castle Rock experience in a single book.

## The Hulu series (2018–2019)

*Castle Rock* the TV series, which ran for two seasons on Hulu starting July 25, 2018, isn't a direct adaptation of any one book — it's an anthology that uses the town and scattered references to King's wider catalog (including the Shawshank prison, which the show places just outside town) to tell new, original stories. Season one starred André Holland, Sissy Spacek, Bill Skarsgård, and Melanie Lynskey, centered on a mysterious prisoner found in a cage beneath Shawshank. Season two shifted focus to a young Annie Wilkes, played by Lizzy Caplan — a prequel of sorts to *Misery* — alongside Tim Robbins reprising a version of Pop Merrill. The show was canceled after season two, so it ends without wrapping up every thread it opens, but it's worth watching for how much King trivia it packs into the background even when it's telling its own story.

## Should you read all of it?

You don't need to read every Castle Rock work to enjoy any single one of them — *Cujo* and *The Body* in particular work perfectly as standalones and are two of King's most acclaimed books regardless of setting. But if you've read a few King novels already and are looking for the next thread to pull, Castle Rock is one of the most rewarding, because the connections are small and human-scale rather than cosmic: a sheriff who shows up again, a family name that keeps recurring, a town that remembers what happened to it even when the books move on.
