Stephen King has written gods, demons, and a clown that's been feeding on a town for centuries. His scariest character is none of those. She's a heavyset, church-going former nurse in rural Colorado who calls swearing "dirty birdy" language and keeps a pig named Misery. *[Misery](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Misery+Stephen+King&tag=YOURTAG-20)*, published in 1987, is the novel that proved King didn't need the supernatural to write something genuinely terrifying — he just needed a character who was completely, believably human, and completely without a ceiling on what she'd do next.

## The setup

Paul Sheldon is a bestselling novelist who made his fortune on a series of Victorian romance novels starring a character named Misery Chastain — books he's grown to resent even as they've made him rich, because they keep him from being taken seriously as a "real" writer. Driving through a Colorado blizzard after finishing the manuscript for a new, more literary novel, Paul crashes his car. He's pulled from the wreckage by Annie Wilkes, a former nurse living alone nearby, who tells him she's his "number one fan."

At first that looks like extraordinary luck. Annie splints his shattered legs, feeds him, and keeps him alive through the kind of injuries that should have killed him on that mountain road. Then she reads the manuscript of his new novel — and, separately, an early copy of the newest Misery book, in which Paul has killed off the character to finally be free of the series. Annie is not a casual fan. She is unraveled by both discoveries, and Paul realizes, from his hospital bed inside her house, that no one knows where he is, and that the woman keeping him alive is the only person who can also end him.

## What Annie actually does to him

What makes *Misery* land as hard as it does is how patiently King builds Annie's control before he lets the violence start. She isn't a masked killer breaking down a door — she's already inside the house, already the only source of food, pain medication, and human contact Paul has. The horror is procedural: she decides what he eats, when his pain medicine comes, whether he's allowed a typewriter, whether he's allowed to live through the night. Every scene is a negotiation Paul cannot actually win, because Annie is the one who gets to change the terms.

When Annie discovers Paul has been sneaking out of his room, she punishes him with the novel's most notorious scene: she lays a board across his ankles and takes an axe to one of his feet, then cauterizes the wound herself with a blowtorch, all with the flat, matter-of-fact affect of a nurse performing a procedure. Later in the book, after another provocation, she takes one of his thumbs with an electric knife. King doesn't write these as set-piece horror kills — he writes them as Annie calmly correcting behavior, which is exactly why they're so hard to shake. Between punishments, she forces Paul to write an entirely new novel, *Misery's Return*, resurrecting the character he'd killed off, on a typewriter that's missing the letter N — a detail King uses throughout the book as a constant, physical reminder of exactly how little control Paul has over anything, including his own sentences.

## What makes her Stephen King's scariest villain

Part of it is simply that Annie Wilkes could exist. She's not possessed, cursed, or supernatural in any way — King has said directly that he wanted to write a story about addiction and obsessive fandom without a single fantastical element to soften it, and Annie is the result. Cocaine was reportedly on King's mind while he wrote her; he's described Annie as, in part, a stand-in for his own addictions during the 1980s, the "number one fan" who controls a writer's fate and won't let go. That's part of why the book still reads as more personal than most of his other work — it's a horror novel about a writer being tortured by the thing keeping him alive, which is a fairly direct metaphor for what addiction actually does.

The other part is that Annie's menace is domestic and procedural rather than theatrical. She prays. She has moods. She's lonely, and genuinely believes she loves Paul, right up until the moments she's maiming him. King gives her a backstory — implied history as a suspected serial killer of hospital patients, including infants, that predates the novel — but the book never turns her into a movie monster who monologues about her plan. She's terrifying because she's recognizable: an unstable person with total situational power over someone who has no way to leave, escalating a little further every time she's disappointed. There's no lair to escape, no ritual to perform against her. Paul's only real weapon, for most of the book, is his own writing — using the manuscript itself to manage and stall her, chapter by chapter, which makes *Misery* as much a story about the power of storytelling as it is a horror novel.

## The ending

Paul eventually turns the tables using the one advantage he still has: he understands narrative, and Annie needs the story he's writing more than she needs anything else. He stages a scene using props from his own manuscript — burning pages to lure her close, then attacking her with, among other things, her own typewriter — and in the novel's climax kills her after a prolonged, brutal struggle, though not before she nearly kills him first. He survives, physically ruined and permanently changed by the experience, and the book ends with Paul newly sober, still writing, still occasionally seeing Annie's face in crowds. The trauma doesn't fully resolve — which is very much the point.

## The adaptations

*[Misery (1990)](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Misery+1990+Blu-ray&tag=YOURTAG-20)*, directed by Rob Reiner from a screenplay by William Goldman, is the definitive adaptation and remains the only Stephen King film to win an acting Oscar: Kathy Bates took Best Actress for her performance as Annie, opposite James Caan as Paul. The film softens the book's most graphic violence — most notably, the infamous "hobbling" scene is changed from an axe amputation to Annie breaking Paul's ankles with a sledgehammer, a choice made to keep the film R-rated and to leave Paul mobile enough for the final confrontation. It's a significant change, but Bates's performance keeps the film's Annie every bit as frightening as King's, largely by underplaying her: the warmth never fully drops, even in the violence, which is what makes her so unsettling on screen.

Misery also had a life on stage: a Broadway adaptation written by William Goldman opened in 2015 at the Broadhurst Theatre, starring Bruce Willis as Paul and Laurie Metcalf as Annie, in Willis's Broadway debut. It ran for about three months and leaned on Metcalf's performance to carry Annie's shifts between devotion and rage in a much more contained, two-hander format than the film.

## Where Annie ranks

Annie Wilkes routinely tops lists of King's best villains for a reason few of his other antagonists can match: she requires no suspension of disbelief. Pennywise, Randall Flagg, and the Overlook Hotel all ask you to believe in something beyond the world as it is. Annie only asks you to believe that an isolated house, a badly timed car accident, and one person's total psychological unraveling are enough — which, unfortunately, they are.
