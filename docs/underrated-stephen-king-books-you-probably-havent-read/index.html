Underrated Stephen King Books You Probably Haven't Read

2026-09-21

Ask a casual reader to name a Stephen King book and you'll get It, The Shining, maybe Misery or Carrie if they've seen the movies. That's fair — those are genuinely his best-known work, and a lot of it's earned. But King has published more than 65 novels, and the ones that never got a blockbuster adaptation tend to fall out of the conversation entirely, regardless of how good they are. Some of his most controlled, most personal, and most purely enjoyable books live in that overlooked tier.

Here are eight that deserve more readers than they get, roughly in the order a King fan looking for something new should try them.

Bag of Bones (1998)

If you only read one book off this list, make it this one. Bag of Bones won the Bram Stoker Award, the World Fantasy Award nomination circuit, and the British Fantasy Award for Best Novel in 1999 — critical validation that most of King's more famous books never bothered chasing, because they didn't need to. It follows Mike Noonan, a bestselling novelist paralyzed by writer's block four years after his wife's sudden death, who retreats to the family's lake house in western Maine and gets pulled into both a custody battle over a local child and a slow-building ghost story rooted in the town's buried racial history.

It's King doing full gothic-romance mode — Southern Gothic transplanted to Maine, essentially — and it's one of his most atmospheric, patient books. If you like The Shining's slow-burn dread more than its jump scares, this is the closest King ever got to repeating that trick without repeating the story.

Lisey's Story (2006)

King himself has called Lisey's Story his favorite of his own novels, and it's easy to see why once you know it's the most autobiographical thing he's written. Lisey Landon is the widow of a famous, difficult novelist, and two years after his death she's finally sorting through his office when she starts uncovering a private mythology he built to survive childhood trauma — a "boo'ya moon," a language only the two of them shared, and a family secret her husband's estate is now circling like vultures.

It's less a horror novel than a portrait of a marriage told through grief, with a fantastical private world stitched into it. Readers expecting monster-of-the-week King tend to bounce off it; readers who come to it wanting to understand what King thinks a creative marriage actually costs tend to rank it near the top of his catalog. It was adapted into an 8-part Apple TV+ miniseries in 2021 with Julianne Moore, though the show came and went with far less buzz than the book deserved.

The Girl Who Loved Tom Gordon (1999)

At under 400 pages, this is one of King's shortest and leanest novels, and almost a genre exercise: nine-year-old Trisha McFarland wanders off the Appalachian Trail during a family hike and spends the rest of the book lost in the Maine woods, alone, with nothing but a Walkman tuned to Red Sox games and her fandom for relief pitcher Tom Gordon keeping her sane. There's a supernatural thread running underneath — something may or may not be stalking her through the trees — but the book's real power is in how completely King commits to a child's-eye survival story, panic and hunger and hallucination rendered with total conviction.

It's a good pick if you want to see King work in miniature, without a 700-page sprawl or an ensemble cast. It's also one of the few King books that reads as genuinely appropriate for a strong middle-grade reader, despite the dread.

Duma Key (2008)

Set on a fictional stretch of the Florida Keys, Duma Key follows Edgar Freemantle, a building contractor who loses an arm and nearly his mind in a construction-site accident, then relocates south to recover. He picks up painting as therapy, mostly to keep from losing his temper and his marriage entirely — and his paintings start showing him things that haven't happened yet, tied to a decades-old tragedy on the island he's moved to.

It's one of King's only novels set outside his usual New England territory, and the change of scenery does real work — Duma Key's heat and isolation replace Maine's cold and superstition as the backdrop for dread, and King's writing about art and phantom-limb pain (he'd broken his own hip and had a difficult recovery a few years earlier) is some of his most physically specific work.

Rose Madder (1995)

Rose Madder opens with Rosie Daniels escaping fourteen years of marriage to an abusive, increasingly unhinged police detective, and the first third of the book — just Rosie building a new, anonymous life in a strange city — is some of King's best non-supernatural writing, tense in a completely different register than his usual monsters. Then she pawns her wedding ring for a painting of a woman in a rose-colored gown standing in a ruined temple, the painting turns out to be a doorway, and the book shifts into a stranger, more mythic register for its back half.

It's uneven in a way that's kept it out of most "best of" lists — the fantastical second half doesn't land as cleanly as the grounded opening — but the character work on Rosie and the portrait of coercive control in the marriage are genuinely strong, and it's worth reading for that alone.

From a Buick 8 (2002)

A Pennsylvania State Police troop keeps a car locked in a shed behind their barracks — a Buick Roadmaster that isn't really a car at all, just something that looks like one, occasionally "breathing" and occasionally opening a door to somewhere else. From a Buick 8 is told in pieces, troopers taking turns explaining the car's twenty-year history to the grown son of a colleague killed in an unrelated accident, and it's deliberately, almost stubbornly anticlimactic — there's no final explanation, no defeating the thing, just people living for decades next to something incomprehensible and learning to manage it rather than understand it.

That restraint is exactly why it's underrated: readers expecting a monster-car horror novel in the vein of Christine are disappointed, but it's really a book about the limits of explanation and the particular grief of losing a parent to something sudden and senseless, with the car as metaphor more than plot engine.

Joyland (2013)

King's second novel for the pulp-revival imprint Hard Case Crime, Joyland is a coming-of-age crime novel set in a 1973 North Carolina amusement park, where college student Devin Jones takes a summer job as a carny and gets pulled into the decades-old unsolved murder of a young woman on the park's haunted-house ride. There's a ghost, technically, and a psychic child, but the book is much more interested in first heartbreak, carny culture and lingo, and the particular ache of a summer that changes you — closer in spirit to The Body (the novella behind Stand By Me) than to any of King's horror.

It was never given a wide paperback push the way King's Scribner novels are, so it's stayed a favorite mostly among readers who specifically sought out the Hard Case Crime editions — which is a shame, because it's one of the most purely pleasurable, fastest reads in his whole bibliography.

Gwendy's Button Box (2017)

Co-written with the late Richard Chizmar, this slim novella is set in King's Castle Rock and follows twelve-year-old Gwendy Peterson, who's given a mysterious box covered in buttons by a stranger in a black hat — press the right button and you get a small treat; press the wrong one and something in the world quietly, terribly changes. It's a Castle Rock story in the tradition of Needful Things — a supernatural object as a test of restraint and character — but compressed into under 200 pages, with none of the sprawl King's full-length Castle Rock books carry.

It kicked off a full trilogy (with Chizmar largely taking over the later books), but the original novella is the tightest and best entry point, and a genuinely good answer if you want to try King's Castle Rock mythology without committing to a 700-page novel first.

Why these never broke through

None of these eight got the adaptation that made It, The Shining, or Pet Sematary into cultural shorthand — the closest is Lisey's Story's quiet 2021 Apple TV+ run, which came and went without much notice. That's really the whole explanation: King's reputation with casual readers is built almost entirely on movie and TV visibility, and the books that never got a marquee adaptation slide out of the conversation no matter how well they're written or reviewed on release. If you've already read the greatest hits and are looking for where to go next, these are the deep cuts worth tracking down — and Bag of Bones in particular deserves to be ranked with his best work, not filed under "the rest."
