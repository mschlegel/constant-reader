Doctor Sleep: Movie vs. Book Ending Compared

2026-10-12

Spoilers follow for the novel and the 2019 film, including both endings.

Stephen King's Doctor Sleep came with a built-in problem no other King sequel has: its 2013 publication meant catching up to not one but two The Shining canons — King's 1977 novel, where the Overlook Hotel burns to the ground, and Stanley Kubrick's 1980 film, where it doesn't. When writer-director Mike Flanagan adapted Doctor Sleep for Warner Bros. in 2019, he had to pick a side, and the choice he made — reopening a hotel the book had already destroyed — is the single biggest change between the two versions, and it reshapes everything about how the story ends.

The setup is close in both versions

Both book and film pick up with Danny Torrance grown into Dan (Ewan McGregor in the film), a drifting alcoholic who inherited his father Jack's drinking problem along with his psychic "shining" ability. Dan gets sober, settles in New Hampshire, and takes a job at a hospice, where he uses his gift to ease dying patients in their final moments — earning the nickname that gives the book its title from a cat, Azzie, who senses death coming the same way Dan does.

Years later, Dan forms a long-distance psychic bond with Abra Stone (Kyliegh Curran), a child whose shining is even stronger than his own. Abra's gift draws the attention of the True Knot, a group of quasi-immortal predators led by Rose the Hat (Rebecca Ferguson) who drift around the country in RVs, torturing and killing children with the shine to feed on the psychic "steam" their deaths release. When the Knot murders a boy named Bradley Trevor and Abra witnesses it happen psychically, she becomes their next target — and Dan, along with his friend Billy Freeman (Cliff Curtis), has to help her survive. Up through this point, Flanagan's film tracks the novel closely, trimming subplots but keeping the core relationships and stakes intact.

Where they split: the Overlook itself

The novel is explicit that the Overlook Hotel no longer exists — it burned down in the climax of The Shining when its boiler exploded, a detail King wrote specifically so his own canon wouldn't contradict itself. So in the book, Dan and Abra's final confrontation with Rose happens on the hotel's empty, overgrown grounds, now the site of a campground, where Dan draws on the residual psychic energy left behind by the building's violent history even though the structure itself is gone.

The film throws that detail out. Flanagan's Overlook is still standing, abandoned and decaying but structurally intact — explicitly built to visually match Kubrick's version, down to the Gold Room and the hedge maze, so longtime fans of the 1980 movie would recognize it instantly. That's a direct break from King's own text, and it's the clearest sign of which influence Flanagan prioritized: the film treats Kubrick's Overlook, not King's burned-down ruin, as the "real" one. It's a choice King himself reportedly signed off on, since the whole point was finally reconciling his story with the version of The Shining most audiences actually know.

Rose the Hat's defeat

In the novel, Dan and Billy corner Rose using Dan's connection to a dying woman named Concetta (Abra's great-grandmother), whose terminal cancer gives Dan access to a particularly toxic form of "steam" that he uses as a weapon against her. Rose is ultimately killed in a physical struggle — pushed from a platform to her death — a comparatively grounded, almost mundane end for a villain who spends the whole book as a near-immortal threat.

The film's version is bigger and stranger, built entirely around the hotel. Dan lures Rose into the Overlook, knowing its restless ghosts — including the woman from Room 237 and the Grady twins — are still there and still hungry. He unlocks the mental "boxes" he built as a child to contain the hotel's spirits (established earlier in both the book and the 1980 film), unleashing them to overwhelm and ultimately destroy Rose. It's a smart structural move: it gives the climax a reason to need the Overlook, something the book's version never required.

Dan's fate: the biggest change

This is where the film diverges hardest from its source. In King's novel, Dan survives. He defeats the True Knot, continues mentoring Abra, and the book ends with him very much alive, having made peace with his father's legacy without repeating it.

The film kills him. After the fight with Rose, Dan deliberately re-triggers the Overlook's boiler — the same mechanical failure that ended the hotel in King's original Shining novel but that Kubrick's film never depicted — and dies in the resulting explosion, sacrificing himself so Abra can get out and the hotel can finally, definitively be destroyed. Flanagan has said in interviews that he wanted Dan to step aside and hand the story off to Abra the way Dick Hallorann once mentored young Danny, and that giving Dan a death scene let the film pay off the boiler-explosion ending from the 1977 novel that Kubrick had skipped entirely — closing a 40-year loop in a single image. Dan's ghost lingers afterward in a scene that mirrors how Tony and Hallorann guided Danny as a boy, now guiding Abra in turn.

A smaller but telling change: Dick Hallorann

Readers of the book know Dick Hallorann survives the original Overlook siege and goes on to mentor young Danny for years afterward, eventually dying of natural causes mentioned only in passing. Kubrick's 1980 film changed that, killing Hallorann at Jack's hands with an axe the moment he arrives to help. Flanagan's Doctor Sleep sides with Kubrick here too: the film's flashbacks treat Hallorann's death at the hotel as settled fact, folding that piece of movie-only canon into the story rather than the book's original version where he lives. It's a small detail compared to the ending, but it's consistent with the film's overall approach — when King's book and Kubrick's film disagree, the movie picks Kubrick.

Why Flanagan made these changes

Flanagan was explicit that Doctor Sleep the film needed to function as a sequel to both the novel and Kubrick's movie at once, since most of its audience knew the story through the film rather than the book. Keeping the Overlook standing let him stage a climax inside the most iconic, recognizable sets in horror-movie history instead of an empty campground, and killing Dan let the film deliver the father-figure's-sacrifice ending that Kubrick's version never got to use. Critics were split on whether the detour into full Kubrick homage served the story or overshadowed it, but the structural logic holds together: every major change traces back to the single decision to let the hotel survive.

Should you read it, watch it, or both?

Read the novel for King's actual argument — that addiction and trauma can be survived and don't have to repeat themselves across generations, which is why Dan living matters to the book's point. Watch the film for the version built to pay off Kubrick's imagery, with a much higher-stakes, more operatic finale.
