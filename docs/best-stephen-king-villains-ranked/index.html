The Best Stephen King Villains, Ranked

2026-08-10

Stephen King's best villains rarely win because they're powerful. They win because they understand exactly which door in a person to push on — grief, loneliness, addiction, small-town boredom, the need to be needed. The monsters with claws and teeth are the ones you remember first, but the ones without them are usually the ones that stay with you.

Here's how his most memorable antagonists stack up, counting down to the one that's hardest to shake.

10. Big Jim Rennie (Under the Dome)

No supernatural powers, no shapeshifting — just a used-car-salesman selectman who turns a small Maine town into a dictatorship the moment an impenetrable dome cuts it off from the outside world. Rennie is King doing pure political horror: the villain isn't the dome, it's how fast ordinary institutions collapse once someone confident and amoral decides to fill the power vacuum.

9. The Trashcan Man (The Stand)

Donald Merwin Elbert — pyromaniac, outcast, and eventually Randall Flagg's most devoted and most unstable follower — is less a mastermind than a walking consequence. He's what happens when someone broken by cruelty finds a "family" that only wants him for his talent for destruction. His arc, ending with a payload of nuclear warheads he delivers straight to Las Vegas, is one of King's bleakest statements about who violence recruits.

8. Henry Bowers (It)

Every King villain list eventually has to reckon with the fact that the scariest thing in It almost isn't Pennywise — it's Henry Bowers, the teenage bully whose casual cruelty toward the Losers' Club is entirely human and entirely plausible. Pennywise manipulates him, but doesn't have to work hard; Henry's viciousness (and his father's) was already there. He's proof that King's monsters usually need a human on-ramp.

7. Greg Stillson (The Dead Zone)

A traveling insurance salesman turned populist congressman turned, in protagonist Johnny Smith's psychic vision, a president who starts a nuclear war — Stillson is King's most direct warning about charisma without conscience. What makes him effective isn't menace, it's likability; he charms an entire town before anyone notices what he actually is. Published in 1979, the book's central fear — an unqualified demagogue riding personality to power — has aged in a way King probably didn't expect.

6. Kurt Barlow ('Salem's Lot)

King's first great vampire, styled closer to Nosferatu than to Dracula's seductive charm, Barlow is pure, old-world predation dropped into a sleepy Maine town — a deliberate rebuttal to the romantic vampire archetype. The novel's real horror is watching the town of Jerusalem's Lot turn undead literally house by house, neighbor by neighbor, until almost no one is left to notice. The 2024 film adaptation, with Alexander Ward as Barlow, leaned into the character's inhuman, insect-like menace rather than any charm at all.

5. Margaret White (Carrie)

King's debut novel gave him one of his most disturbing villains before he'd even settled into his career, and she isn't the demonic force outside the house — she's the mother inside it. Margaret White's religious fanaticism, her prayer closet, and her genuine belief that abusing her daughter is righteous made her a template King returned to again and again: the domestic monster who thinks she's the hero of the story. Piper Laurie's Oscar-nominated performance in the 1976 film, and Julianne Moore's in the 2013 remake, both leaned hard into that terrifying sincerity.

4. Jack Torrance (The Shining)

Jack Torrance is the rare King villain who starts as a genuinely sympathetic protagonist — a recovering alcoholic writer trying to hold his family together — before the Overlook Hotel's influence and his own buried rage curdle him into something monstrous. That slow corruption is what makes him more frightening than a hotel full of ghosts: the book is explicit that the Overlook needed something already broken in Jack to work with. If you want the details of how the novel's version of that unraveling differs from what Jack Nicholson does on screen, see our breakdown of why King hated Kubrick's adaptation.

3. Pennywise / It (It)

It takes the shape of a clown because a clown is what a child's mind can hold onto — its actual nature, revealed late in the book, is something far older and stranger that just happens to feed on fear. Pennywise endures as King's signature monster because the concept is so adaptable: it becomes whatever a given victim is most afraid of, which means every reader's version of it is slightly different. Tim Curry defined the role for a generation in 1990; Bill Skarsgård reinvented it for the 2017/2019 films and is back playing a young, pre-Losers'-Club version of the entity in HBO's prequel series Welcome to Derry, which premiered in October 2025. For the full breakdown of what the ending means, read our It plot summary and ending explained.

2. Annie Wilkes (Misery)

No supernatural backstory, no cosmic origin — just a nurse with a shelf full of scrapbooks and a very specific, very personal definition of devotion. Annie Wilkes holds novelist Paul Sheldin captive after a car crash, "cares" for him, and turns violent the instant his writing threatens to disappoint her. What makes her one of King's scariest creations is how mundane the horror stays: no monster ever needs to show up, because obsessive fandom curdled into control is already enough. Misery won King a devoted following of its own, and Kathy Bates's 1990 performance — which won her the Academy Award for Best Actress — remains the gold standard for adapting a King villain to screen without softening a single edge.

1. Randall Flagg

Nobody else on this list gets to be the answer twice. Randall Flagg is the closest thing King's fiction has to a unifying dark force — the antagonist of The Stand, the man in black chased across the desert in The Gunslinger, and a recurring presence across the wider Dark Tower multiverse, showing up under different names (Walter, Marten Broadcloft, the Ageless Stranger) in book after book. What makes Flagg genuinely unsettling isn't raw power, it's persuasion: after a plague wipes out 99% of humanity in The Stand, he doesn't conquer the survivors so much as get elected by the worst impulses in them, building Las Vegas into a fascist state out of people who chose him. He's shown up on screen played by Jamey Sheridan in the 1994 miniseries and Alexander Skarsgård in the 2020 CBS All Access adaptation, and King has kept threading him through new books for decades — there's no other character in his catalog with that kind of reach.

Runners-up worth knowing

A ranked top ten leaves out a lot of good ones. Percy Wetmore (The Green Mile) is petty cruelty in a prison guard's uniform. Mrs. Carmody in The Mist turns religious panic into its own kind of monster once the real ones show up. And Christine, the murderous 1958 Plymouth Fury, proves King can make a villain out of literally anything if the metaphor is sharp enough — in her case, toxic obsession dressed up as a muscle car.

What actually makes a King villain work

The pattern across this list holds up: King's most enduring villains are rarely scary because of what they can do. They're scary because of how recognizable the shape of them is — a mother who thinks cruelty is love, a fan who thinks control is devotion, a leader who thinks charisma excuses everything. Even the two outright supernatural entries at the top of this list, Pennywise and Flagg, work by finding real human weaknesses first and exploiting those before anything otherworldly kicks in. That's the throughline of King's horror in general: the monster is rarely the whole story, and it's almost never the scariest part of it.
