Different Seasons: Guide to the Four Novellas (Shawshank, Apt Pupil, and More)

Published by Viking on August 27, 1982, Different Seasons is one of the strangest entries in Stephen King's bibliography for a simple reason: it's barely a horror book. It's a collection of four novellas, each one tied to a season and a mood, and only one of the four has anything supernatural in it at all. King wrote it partly to prove to himself and everyone else that he could do more than horror — and the results include some of the most acclaimed film adaptations his work has ever produced.

If you only know this book through its movies, it's worth understanding what's actually in it, because the collection as a whole is a genuinely different reading experience than any of the individual films suggest on their own.

What's in the collection

Each novella carries its own subtitle tying it to a season, which is where the book's title comes from:

  • "Hope Springs Eternal": Rita Hayworth and Shawshank Redemption — adapted as The Shawshank Redemption (1994)
  • "Summer of Corruption": Apt Pupil — adapted as Apt Pupil (1998)
  • "Fall from Innocence": The Body — adapted as Stand by Me (1986)
  • "A Winter's Tale": The Breathing Method — never adapted

Three out of four became films, which is an unusually high hit rate for any single book, let alone one that isn't horror. The fourth, "The Breathing Method," remains the collection's most overlooked piece simply because there's no movie pulling readers toward it.

"Rita Hayworth and Shawshank Redemption"

Andy Dufresne, a mild-mannered banker, is convicted of murdering his wife and her lover — a crime he insists he didn't commit — and sentenced to two consecutive life terms at Shawshank State Penitentiary. The novella is narrated by Red, a fellow inmate who can get contraband items smuggled in, including the Rita Hayworth poster that gives the story its title. Over nearly two decades, Andy quietly becomes indispensable to the prison's corrupt administration by helping the warden launder money, all while running a secret decades-long project the reader — and Red — don't fully understand until the end: tunneling out through his cell wall with a rock hammer, behind that same poster, one inch at a time.

The novella and Frank Darabont's film follow the same basic shape, but the film expands Andy and Red's friendship considerably and changes several details, including how Andy handles the warden's money and what happens to the men who wronged him. "Apt Pupil"

The darkest story in the collection by a wide margin. Todd Bowden, an outwardly all-American teenager, discovers that an elderly neighbor, Arthur Denker, is actually Kurt Dussander, a former SS officer who ran a concentration camp and has been living under a false identity for decades. Instead of turning him in, Todd blackmails Dussander into recounting his atrocities in graphic detail, feeding a fascination with cruelty that Todd doesn't understand about himself yet. What follows is a slow mutual corruption: Dussander starts reawakening his own capacity for violence, and Todd's fascination curdles into something closer to what made Dussander dangerous in the first place. Both men spiral — Todd toward the edges of his own composure, Dussander toward a string of killings — until the novella's genuinely bleak ending, in which Todd's unraveling turns violent and the story refuses any easy moral cleanup.

Bryan Singer's 1998 film adaptation stars Ian McKellen as Dussander and Brad Renfro as Todd, with a supporting cast including Bruce Davison, Elias Koteas, David Schwimmer, and Joshua Jackson. The film softens a few of the novella's most disturbing beats — King's version goes further into Todd's escalating cruelty toward animals and people before the ending — but keeps the central, uncomfortable premise intact: that evil doesn't need a monster, just a willing audience.

"The Body"

Four 12-year-old boys in the small town of Castle Rock, Maine — Gordie, Chris, Teddy, and Vern — set out on a two-day hike along the railroad tracks after hearing that the body of a missing local boy is lying in the woods a few towns over. It's structured as a story-within-a-story: an adult Gordie, now a writer, is narrating his memory of that hike decades later, and the novella is as much about that framing — how it feels to look back on the specific, unrepeatable summer when childhood started ending — as it is about the walk itself. Along the way the boys deal with a junkyard dog, a leech-infested swamp, a train they barely outrun, and Chris's older brother's gang of small-time delinquents who are also after the body. When they finally find it, the moment is quieter and sadder than the adventure leading up to it, and the group returns home changed in ways none of them can fully name yet.

Rob Reiner's 1986 film Stand by Me renamed the story and cast Wil Wheaton, River Phoenix, Corey Feldman, and Jerry O'Connell as the four boys, with Kiefer Sutherland as the gang leader Ace and Richard Dreyfuss narrating as the adult Gordie. It's widely considered one of the best King adaptations ever made, faithful to the novella's tone and mostly to its plot, and it's the film most responsible for cementing King's reputation as more than a horror novelist. "The Breathing Method"

The odd one out, and the only piece with a genuinely supernatural element. A lawyer named David Adley is invited to a private, invitation-only men's club in Manhattan where members tell each other unusual stories. There he hears the tale of Sandra Stansfield, an unmarried pregnant woman in the 1930s — a scandalous position for the era — who trains obsessively in breathing techniques to get through labor safely, whatever happens. What happens is a car accident on her way to the hospital that decapitates her, and in the novella's genuinely uncanny final image, her body keeps performing the breathing exercises and delivers the baby successfully even after death, through sheer force of will. The club itself is left deliberately mysterious — its rules, its membership, and the building's stranger back rooms are hinted at rather than explained, seemingly as an opening for a longer story King never wrote.

No film or TV adaptation of "The Breathing Method" exists, which is a shame — its short length and single unforgettable set piece would translate well, and its rarity relative to the other three is exactly why it's worth reading if you've only seen the movies.

Why this collection matters

Different Seasons was King's deliberate pivot away from being typecast as strictly a horror writer, following a run of novels — Carrie, 'Salem's Lot, The Shining, The Stand, Cujo — that had defined him almost entirely by genre. Three of these four stories have nothing supernatural in them at all, and the fourth uses its supernatural element as more of a closing grace note than a plot engine. What holds the collection together instead is King's recurring interest in ordinary people under sustained pressure — a wrongly convicted man, a corrupted teenager, four boys facing mortality for the first time, a woman facing death itself — and how character reveals itself over the long haul rather than in a single scare. It's also the book that proved King's instincts translated to prestige filmmaking as well as horror, something the three surviving adaptations demonstrated emphatically enough that Hollywood kept coming back for more.

Should you read it?

Yes, especially if you've seen one or more of the films and assumed you already knew the stories. Each novella holds up as prose in ways the runtime of a two-hour film can't fully capture — Red's narration in "Shawshank," Todd and Dussander's toxic dynamic in "Apt Pupil," and the framing device around "The Body" all do work on the page that the adaptations can only approximate. And "The Breathing Method" is worth reading precisely because there's no shortcut to it.
