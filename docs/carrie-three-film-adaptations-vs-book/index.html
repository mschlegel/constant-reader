Carrie: Comparing All Three Film Adaptations to the Book

2026-09-28

Carrie, published in 1974, was Stephen King's first novel, and it's been adapted for the screen more times than almost anything else in his catalog: a 1976 theatrical film, a short-lived 1988 Broadway musical, a 2002 TV movie, a 2013 theatrical remake, and even an off-Broadway parody. The three film versions — 1976, 2002, and 2013 — each take a different approach to the same premise, and each changes the ending in a different way. Here's how all three compare to King's original novel.

The novel, briefly

Carrie is the story of Carrie White, a bullied, sheltered teenager raised by Margaret, her fanatically religious single mother, in the fictional town of Chamberlain, Maine. Carrie discovers she has telekinesis right around the time she gets her first period in the girls' locker room — an event her classmates mock her for, unaware that nobody ever explained menstruation to her at home. A cruel prank at prom, engineered by mean-girl Chris Hargenson and her boyfriend Billy Nolan, pushes Carrie past her breaking point, and she uses her powers to kill nearly everyone at the dance and burn much of the town to the ground before going home to confront her mother and, ultimately, die herself.

What's easy to forget, because none of the films fully replicate it, is that the novel isn't told in a straight line. King structured it as a pieced-together record after the fact — fictional excerpts from a book called My Name Is Susan Snell, newspaper clippings, and testimony from "The White Commission," a fictional state investigation into what became known as "the Black Prom." The reader knows from the first chapter that something catastrophic is coming; the suspense is in watching it arrive. That documentary, after-the-fact framing is arguably the hardest thing about the book to adapt, and none of the three films really try.

Carrie (1976): Brian De Palma's original

Directed by Brian De Palma from a screenplay by Lawrence D. Cohen, the original Carrie opened in theaters on November 3, 1976, with Sissy Spacek in the title role. The supporting cast is stacked with actors who went on to bigger things: Piper Laurie as Margaret White, John Travolta as Billy Nolan, Amy Irving as Sue Snell, William Katt as Tommy Ross, Nancy Allen as Chris Hargenson, P.J. Soles as Norma, and Betty Buckley as the sympathetic gym teacher, Miss Collins.

De Palma's film drops the White Commission framing entirely and tells the story as a straightforward linear narrative, which makes it more of a conventional teen-horror movie than the novel's slow-motion catastrophe report. It also scales back the destruction dramatically: in the book, Carrie levels a meaningful chunk of Chamberlain and the death toll runs into the hundreds. The film, working with a modest budget, largely confines the carnage to the gymnasium — practical for a mid-1970s production, but a real tonal difference from the book's scope.

A few other changes stand out. Margaret White in the novel is described as overweight and plain; Piper Laurie's Margaret is conventionally attractive, with auburn hair and a softer, almost genteel manner that makes her fanaticism land differently on screen. Mrs. Snell, Sue's mother, barely appears in the book but gets a substantial supporting role in the film — reportedly because De Palma discovered that Amy Irving's real-life mother, Priscilla Pointer, was also an actress, and expanded the part for her.

The film's most famous invention isn't in the book at all: the final jump scare, where Sue Snell dreams she's laying flowers at Carrie's grave and a bloodied arm shoots up out of the ground to grab her. It has nothing to do with King's ending, but it became one of the most imitated moments in horror-movie history — and, notably, King himself has said he prefers the film's ending to his own novel's.

Carrie (2002): the NBC TV movie

The 2002 version, directed by David Carson from a script by Bryan Fuller (later known for Hannibal and Pushing Daisies), aired on NBC on November 4, 2002, and drew about 12 million viewers. Angela Bettis played Carrie, with Patricia Clarkson as Margaret White, and a supporting cast that included Emilie de Ravin, Katharine Isabelle, Rena Sofer, and David Keith.

This version makes a real effort to restore material the 1976 film dropped — it brings back a version of the book's fractured, after-the-fact structure, framing the story through a detective interviewing survivors, and it includes scenes closer to the novel's actual destruction of Chamberlain, including the collapsing gas station explosion. It's arguably the most faithful of the three to the book's plot mechanics, at least until the ending.

The ending is where the 2002 movie departs from King's novel more than either film does: Carrie doesn't die. Sue Snell secretly helps her escape town, and the film closes with Carrie starting a new life in Florida. This wasn't just a softer, TV-friendly choice — the movie was shot as a potential pilot for a series that would have followed Carrie using her powers to help other people, similar in structure to The Incredible Hulk. The series never got picked up, which left this version with an ending that doesn't resolve anything like the book's does.

Carrie (2013): the Kimberly Peirce remake

The 2013 remake, directed by Kimberly Peirce (Boys Don't Cry), opened in theaters on October 18, 2013, with Chloë Grace Moretz as Carrie and Julianne Moore as Margaret White. Gabriella Wilde played Sue Snell, Portia Doubleday played Chris Hargenson, Alex Russell played Billy Nolan, Ansel Elgort played Tommy Ross, and Judy Greer played Miss Desjardin (the gym teacher, renamed from the 1976 film's Miss Collins).

Despite being billed as a fresh adaptation and despite Moretz saying at the time that the film was "kind of going off the book" and going for something "darker and more psychological," the 2013 version actually reuses Lawrence D. Cohen's original 1976 screenplay as its base rather than adapting the novel from scratch. The most notable addition is a new prologue showing Margaret's traumatic home birth of Carrie — a scene that doesn't exist in the book — and a more contemporary bullying angle, with Chris and her friends filming Carrie's locker-room breakdown on a smartphone and posting it online, updating King's 1974 setting for a social-media age.

This version does restore one detail the 1976 film invented rather than took from the book: Margaret's death staged as a "Saint Sebastian" tableau, pinned to a doorway by flying kitchen utensils. That image isn't in King's novel either — in the book, Carrie kills her mother by telekinetically stopping her heart — but it's become such a recognizable piece of the story's screen iconography that the 2013 film brings it back rather than inventing something new. As for the film's own ending, Peirce and the studio reportedly disagreed about how to close it; Moretz has said her preferred version, involving Carrie's arm reaching out of a body bag to grab Sue in a hospital delivery room, was scrapped in favor of a finale closer to De Palma's famous hand-out-of-the-grave shock.

So which one is most faithful?

None of the three films fully captures the novel's documentary structure, and all three change Carrie's ending in some way, even the two that let her die: the scale of destruction, the specific mechanics of Margaret's death, and what happens to Sue Snell afterward all shift from version to version. If you want the closest thing to King's plot beats, the 2002 TV movie gets closer on structure and the town's destruction — but its softened, spinoff-ready ending is the furthest from the book emotionally. If you want the version that best captures the novel's mood of claustrophobic dread and inevitability, most critics and King himself point to the 1976 original, even though it trims the book's scope down to fit a much smaller budget.

The honest answer is that no adaptation has ever really attempted the book's central formal trick — burying the reader in reports and interviews after the fact so that the story's ending is never really in doubt, only its details. Reading the novel is still the only way to get that version of Carrie, and at under 200 pages, it's one of the fastest reads in King's whole catalog.
